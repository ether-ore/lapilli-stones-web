What's Coming

The road ahead for Lapilli Stones

Lapilli Stones is finished in the sense that matters — the game is complete, and every update below is free to anyone who owns it. This page is where the next chapters get written down — and where the ones already built are marked as such. Dates are deliberately absent; the work is done when it's good.

Already Landed

Built, in testing now

  • The companion book, inside the app. Over a hundred and sixty pages on the game — its rules, its shapes, its strategy, and the legend it comes wrapped in — with a contents menu, a page slider, and a memory for where you stopped reading. No purchase, no code, no catch: if you own the game, you own the book.
  • Every game written down. Games are recorded as they're played, in a plain notation you can save, export, and send to another player. Every game recorded from here on becomes part of your history for what follows.
  • First move or second, in your record. The record knows who you played, under which rules, and now whether you opened the game or answered it — because that, not the colour of the stones, is where most players find their weakness.
  • Rematch, with the first move swapped. One tap to play the same opponent again, from the other side of the opening.
  • A computer that sees captures coming. The opponents used to count captures already made, but had no notion of one about to happen — neither a pair of theirs standing in a half-closed bracket, nor one of yours ready to be taken. Both are visible now, under every rule set, and the reflexes that answer a threat will reach for a capture when a capture is the better answer. Found by two players who said the same thing in different words.

Boards and Stones

Next

  • Other boards, other stones — mix them as you like. The carved Roman board and its glass drops are one player's memory of a set bought in 1979, which is a personal taste and not everybody's. So: a proper Go board with its nine star points, black and white stones for those who read diagrams that way, and any combination you please — the board and the stones are separate choices, not bundled sets.

Study Your Games

After that

  • A mirror for your play. The same judge that powers the computer opponents reads back through your recorded games and finds the mistakes you repeat — the capture threat you keep missing, the open three you keep forgiving — and shows whether they're fading as you improve. All of it on your device; your games are read by your own machine and no one else's.
  • Walk back through a finished game. Step move by move through a game you've played, with the computer's read of each position beside you. Turning “I lost” into “I lost here.”
  • A proper end to a game. The final board, the captures, the count of moves — and the things you actually want next: rematch, review, export.
  • A word after the game. Correspondence play deserves the courtesies of the post: a way to say good game, well played, thank you when a match ends, chosen from a short list and sent with your last move. Not a chat — a handful of civil things to say to someone you may never meet, in a game whose own etiquette chapter is about announcing your threats aloud.
  • Your matches, on the board's edge. Correspondence games move out of the settings and onto the main screen — a sidebar list on the bigger screens, a slim strip of name-chips on the phone — so seeing whose move it is never costs a tap. (A tester's suggestion, from the family that tests everything.)

The Practice Board

Later

  • A board with two bowls beside it. Place stones wherever you like, work a position out by hand, and keep a note of what you found — no turns, no opponent, no clock.
  • The book's problems, playable. Positions from the companion book, opened on a real board, with your solutions remembered.
  • A hint that tells you why. Not just where to play, but what it's answering — in the language the book teaches.
Ideas are welcome and often become entries on this page. Nearly every improvement in the game so far started as a message from someone playing it — write to lapilli-stones@proton.me.
